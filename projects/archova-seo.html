ARCHOVA — Weaning a Studio Site Off Paid Search

Type: SEO & content strategy · Scope: Organic growth, measured in GA4 · Window: Jan 2024 – Apr 2025

Archova Visuals sells 3D architectural rendering and animation to architects and designers. Going into 2024, the site depended on paid search for 84% of its sessions and had only ever drawn 3,395 active users total. Organic search was close to nonexistent.

A content strategy built around the questions architects and homeowners actually search for — architectural styles, interior design approaches, HVAC systems, building materials — replaced that spend. Over the following 481 days, paid search fell to eight sessions out of 96,081, and the site pulled in 73,702 active users on its own.

The strategy

Instead of a services page arguing why Archova is worth hiring, the highest-traffic page on the site is an explainer: /what-is-architectural-styles. It pulled 7,086 sessions and 6,486 new users on its own — roughly three times what the homepage drew. The pattern repeats down the list: articles on the future of architecture, HVAC systems made in the USA, modern rustic interior design, geometric shapes in building design, and critical regionalism each brought in over a thousand new users apiece. None of them sell anything directly. They rank, they answer the question someone typed, and a fraction of that reader base finds its way to the services the studio actually offers.

Languages and devices

English-language browsers make up 84.4% of active users. Spanish (1,300), Chinese (1,160), and French (1,119) each cleared a thousand readers with no translated content behind them — a sign the topics travel further than the language they're written in. Reading happens mostly at a desk: 64.9% desktop against 33.6% mobile and 1.5% tablet, with Chrome on Windows as the dominant combination.

Reading the data honestly

  • Nobody comes back. The six-week cohort shows 100% of users active in week 0 and 0% returning in weeks 1–5. The site is an acquisition funnel with no retention behind it.
  • Conversions were never being measured. No key events are configured in the property, so GA4 recorded no conversions across the full 481 days. The 542 form_submit events are the closest thing to a lead count that exists.
  • The form tracking doesn't add up. 542 submissions against 522 starts isn't possible as instrumented, so one of the two events is firing incorrectly and neither number can be trusted yet.
  • Two measurement gaps. A "(not set)" landing page absorbed 8,764 sessions — 9.1% of the total — at 3 seconds of engagement and zero new users, which reads as a tagging gap rather than real traffic. Separately, the 404 page took 1,300 views, up from near zero.
The honest read: the traffic is real and it's earned, but the funnel stops at the article. A 2,527% rise in sessions sits next to a conversion figure nobody can currently verify.
